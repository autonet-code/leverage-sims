# r15a: Evidence for lever round 2 (L2, L4, L5, L6, L8, L9)

Date: 2026-10-01. The web search budget was already used up in this session (200/200), so new evidence comes from direct page fetches (F). Other figures are recalled from the literature without re-checking (M). Read M as medium confidence at best. J means a judgment prior.

## L2 Revenue starvation -> capex
- AI capex runs far ahead of AI revenue. Hyperscaler capex in 2026 is about $650-725B (m7, high confidence). OpenAI revenue was about $13B in 2025 and about $20B ARR in Nov 2025, against about $1.4T of compute commitments over 8 years, a $300B Oracle contract, and projected cumulative losses of about $140B for 2024-29 (Deutsche Bank) (F, Wikipedia OpenAI / AI bubble). The capex-to-AI-revenue ratio is roughly 5-10x (M/J).
- Financing depends on growth expectations. Funding rounds were $40B (Apr 2025), $110-122B (Feb-Apr 2026, $852B post-money), plus Nvidia's $100B circular investment. Datacenter debt is expected to pass $1T by 2028, and about half of the $3T of 2025-28 datacenter spend is projected to come from private credit, much of it rated BBB or junk (F, AI bubble). Wikipedia reports that the KOSPI fell 44% in 40 days in July 2026 on doubts about AI ROI (F, unverified elsewhere).
- Stargate: Bloomberg reported in Aug 2025 that "no funds were raised" against targets, and the Argentina site was still stalled in May 2026 (F). **The reported 2026 collapse of the Abilene expansion financing could not be verified this pass.** Do not cite it until it is checked.
- Capex response to revenue and financing shocks (M): telecom capex fell about 50-60% from 2000 to 2002 when revenue disappointed and the credit window shut. In 2022 Meta held capex roughly flat despite a 70% stock drop. Firms funded from cash flow respond weakly, and firms dependent on external finance respond strongly. In the investment and cash-flow literature (Fazzari-Hubbard-Petersen), the sensitivity is 0.3-0.6 for constrained firms.
- Parameters:
  - Elasticity of frontier-lab capex to expected revenue: 1.0 (range 0.5-2.0). These labs depend on external finance, so a revenue shortfall cuts their financing as well (J, M).
  - Elasticity for hyperscalers: 0.4 (range 0.2-0.7). Their capex is funded from core cash flow, but that core business (ads, cloud, commerce) also shrinks in proportion to the activity that leaves (M).
  - Lag: 0.5-1.5 years (M). Evidence quality: medium on direction, low on magnitude.

## L4 Organized demand withdrawal
| Case | Participation / target | Effect | Src |
|---|---|---|---|
| Montgomery 1955-56 | about 90% of Black riders, who made up 75% of all riders | Serious economic distress for the transit system. Won after 382 days, through a court ruling | F |
| Bud Light 2023 | Small active share of US adults | Brand -25 to -30% at peak, -17.5% by Oct 2023, about -40% persisting into 2025. AB InBev US revenue -13.5%, $26-27B market cap lost | F |
| Target 2023 / 2025 | Diffuse | Comparable sales -5.4% (Q2 2023), about -3% (Q1 2025) | M |
| Chinese boycotts | State-amplified | Japanese car sales in China -40 to -50% (2012). Hyundai China sales about -1/3 and Lotte exits China, about $1.7B losses (2017). H&M China -28% (2021) | M |
| Anti-apartheid | Institutional divestment (53 -> 155 universities, 26 states) | Capital flight of R9.2B in 1985 and the 1985 debt standstill. US imports -35% (1985-87). Business elites met the ANC in 1985. About 5-9 years from peak pressure to settlement | F/M |
| BDS | Diffuse, low participation | Losses estimated at 0.004% of GDP (Reuter) up to $1.4B/yr (leaked government estimate). Effectively nil at the economy level | F |

- Pattern: withdrawal bites when a substitute exists and participation is concentrated. Both conditions hold here, because the network is the substitute. Diffuse moral boycotts with no substitute (BDS) do almost nothing.
- Parameters:
  - Revenue loss to the target per unit of withdrawing share: 0.8 (range 0.5-1.2) when a substitute is available (J, anchored on Bud Light and Montgomery).
  - Persistence after 2 years: 0.6 (range 0.3-0.9) (F, Bud Light).
  - Time for the effect to show: 1-4 weeks (F).
- Caveat: firm revenue is not the same as political concession. Montgomery and apartheid won only with a legal or elite channel on top of the withdrawal.

## L5 Talent
- In Nov 2023, 745 of 770 OpenAI staff (97%) threatened to quit, and the board reversed within 5 days (F). In the short run, talent holds a veto.
- About half of OpenAI's safety researchers left in 2024. Sutskever and Murati left to found rival labs (F). Anthropic (founded 2021 by about 11 people who left OpenAI) reached the frontier in about 2 years (M). Meta paid $1M-$100M+ packages and $14.3B for Scale AI (F). Those prices imply each top researcher is worth roughly 0.1-1% of a lab's progress (J).
- Open-weight models lag the frontier by 3-12 months (m7, M).
- Parameters:
  - Elasticity of the centralized capability rate to its share of top talent: 0.3 (range 0.1-0.5) before R&D is automated, fading to about 0 once AI does most R&D (J).
  - Share of frontier talent willing to move on moral or economic grounds: 10-30% (J. Anchors are the safety exodus at about 50% of one team and the 97% letter as an upper bound on solidarity).
- Evidence quality: low.

## L6 Cost of cracking down on a large share of one's own economy
- China, 2020-23: the Ant IPO ($34.5B raise, $313B valuation) was halted, followed by a ¥7.1B fine (F). Roughly $1T+ of Chinese tech market value was wiped out, the tutoring sector (about $100B) was gutted, and youth unemployment reached 21.3% in 2023 before the easing in 2023 (M). Lesson: an autocracy paid about 5-7% of GDP in equity value to *subordinate* private power. It captured the platforms and did not shut them down.
- China's 2021 crypto ban took China's share of hash rate from about 46% to 0, which later recovered underground to about 15-20% (r14a). The cost was small relative to GDP, so the ban was easy.
- India 2016 demonetisation: 86% of currency by value was voided. GDP growth fell from 8.0% to 7.1%, and the PMI from 54.5 to 46.7. About 1.5M jobs were lost and 82-100+ people died (F). Chodorow-Reich et al. (2020) estimate about -2pp of growth for the quarter (M). The government was re-elected in 2019. **A democracy will impose a shock on most of its economy if it frames the shock as legitimate.**
- US Prohibition: a large industry was banned, enforcement failed, and the ban was repealed after 13 years (M). The Soviet second economy (10-30% of urban income) was tolerated because the state depended on it (m7).
- Capital flight as a cost: South Africa lost R3-9B a year in 1985-88 (F), and Russia saw about $250B of net outflow in 2022 (M).
- Parameters:
  - The direct cost of a full ban scales with network share x (1-2x supply-chain multiplier) (J). Above about 10-20% of GDP, a full ban is implausible in a democracy. **The crackdown form shifts from ban to capture and regulation** rather than the hazard falling to zero.
  - P(form = capture | crackdown, x > 0.15): 0.6-0.8 (J).
  - In autocracies, the incidence of crackdown does not fall with size until the network rivals the regime's own base. Falun Gong was banned at a size larger than the CCP (r14a). Too-big-to-crush evidence is weak for autocracies and moderate for democracies.

## L8 Speed of coordination
- Digital mobilization: Tunisia took 28 days and Egypt 18 days to topple the regime (M). The OpenAI letter took 3 days (F). Ukraine's IT Army was announced on 26 Feb 2022, was carrying out attacks within 2 days, and had about 1,000 volunteers in a week (F). Ukrainian crowdfunding raised hundreds of millions of dollars within weeks (M).
- DAOs: The DAO hack to hard fork took 33 days (F). MakerDAO's emergency votes after Black Thursday took about 1-2 days. Standard on-chain governance takes about 7 days (review + vote + timelock) (M).
- Bureaucracies: US federal rulemaking takes a median of about 1-2 years and major legislation takes months. Crisis exceptions such as the 2020 Fed facilities took days (M).
- Counterpoint: Tufekci (2017) and Chenoweth (2020) find that digitally organized movements scale fast but are fragile, and their success rates fell after 2010 (M).
- Parameters:
  - Speed multiplier on collective response: 3x (range 2-10x) for mobilization and decisions (M/J).
  - Durability penalty: 0.7-1.0 (J). Tying debate to spending may offset it, but that is untested.

## L9 Legitimacy (Chenoweth and Stephan)
- Stephan and Chenoweth (2008), IS 33:1, read from the PDF (F):
  - Under a violent crackdown, nonviolent campaigns are 6.39x more likely to fully succeed than violent ones, and 11.8x more likely to win limited concessions (RRR, N=234).
  - Security force defection gives a 4.44x RRR overall and 46.5x within nonviolent campaigns (small n, wide CI).
  - Defections occurred in 52% of successful nonviolent campaigns and 32% of successful violent ones.
  - Nonviolent methods did *not* significantly raise the probability of defection.
  - **Within nonviolent campaigns, regime violence had no statistically significant effect on outcome.** Backfire is real in specific cases but is not a net average effect.
  - International sanctions had no effect on nonviolent campaigns.
- Success rates: 53% for nonviolent vs 26% for violent (1900-2006). About 65% in the 1990s, about 34% in the 2010s, and lower in the 2020s (F/M). Nonviolent campaigns draw about 4x the participants (F). The 3.5% rule is a rule of thumb (F). Average peak participation since 2010 is 1.3% (m4).
- Parameters:
  - Recruitment multiplier from legitimacy: 1.5x (range 1.0-4x) (F/J).
  - Backfire: mean effect about 0 (range -0.1 to +0.2 on the crackdown success hazard). Do not assume a large positive backfire.
  - Defection uplift: 2-4x, applied only to the human-staffed share of enforcement, so it fades as institutions automate (F, A-level for direction).
  - Use the 2010s success baseline (0.34), not the 0.53 historical figure.
