## Theory and evidence -> model structure
1. Relative deprivation (Gurr 1970). Grievance comes from the gap between expected and actual status. Grievance alone predicts conflict onset weakly (Fearon-Laitin 2003; Collier-Hoeffler 2004): opportunity, state capacity and terrain dominate. So model grievance as necessary, not sufficient, gated by capacity to mobilize and by the state's coercive capacity.
2. Opportunity cost / income shocks. Miguel-Satyanath-Sergenti 2004: a 5 pp growth drop raises civil conflict risk by about 50% the next year. Dube-Vargas 2013: a price collapse in labor-intensive coffee raised violence (opportunity cost), and a price rise in capital-intensive oil raised it too (predation). AI automation combines both: labor income collapses while capital rents concentrate.
3. Youth bulge (Urdal 2006): +1 pp youth share gives about +4% conflict risk. Youth unemployment was about 25-30% before the Arab Spring.
4. In rich democracies, deindustrialization goes mainly to the ballot box (a populist/radical vote), not insurgency: Autor-Dorn-Hanson-Majlesi 2020; Colantone-Stanig 2018; far right +30% after financial crises (Funke-Schularick-Trebesch 2016). US Depression at 25% unemployment: riots and the Bonus Army, no insurgency. Weimar at about 30%: regime collapse through elections.
5. Machine-breaking. Luddites 1811-16: about 12,000 troops deployed, machine-breaking made a capital crime, about 17 hanged at York in 1813, mechanization continued. Swing riots 1830: about 2,000 tried, 252 sentenced to death, 19 executed, about 500 transported. Caprettini-Voth 2020: threshing-machine diffusion causally raised riot odds, and the riots slowed adoption locally. Pattern: sabotage is common, lasts 1-5 yr, is crushed, delays technology locally and never reverses it.
6. Gilets jaunes 2018-19: fuel-tax spark, peak about 282k people, 11 deaths, about 12k arrests. The state conceded about EUR 17B. Concession plus policing faded the movement within about 1 yr.
7. Dissent-repression nexus. Davenport 2007: the "law of coercive responsiveness", states almost always repress perceived threats. Lichbach 1987: consistent repression cuts nonviolent dissent and can shift it to violence; inconsistent repression inflames. Inverted U (Muller-Weede 1990): semi-repressive regimes see the most violence. Moore 1998: substitution between accommodation and repression. Backfire requires visibility plus the possibility of security-force defection.
8. Nonviolent campaigns (Chenoweth-Stephan): success 1900-2006 was about 53% nonviolent vs 26% violent. It fell to about 34% in the 2010s and lower in the 2020s (digital repression, regime learning). Security-force defection is the strongest predictor of success. Automated security removes the defection channel, the key AI-specific change.
9. Insurgency vs high-tech states. Lyall-Wilson 2009: mechanized armies do worse at COIN, and the incumbent win rate fell from about 90% (early 1800s) to about 25% (1976-2005). Fearon 2004: mean civil war about 7 yr; sons-of-the-soil conflicts last longer. RAND (Connable-Libicki 2010): the average insurgency lasts about 10 yr. But those were mostly foreign or weak incumbents without mass surveillance. Digital repression lengthens autocratic survival (Frantz, Kendall-Taylor, Wright 2020). Xinjiang: about 10% of adult Uyghurs detained, birth rates down about 50% in 2015-18 (Zenz), no sustained insurgency after 2014-17.
10. Elites facing hated, unneeded populations. Labor-extractive colonies kept natives alive. Settler colonies that needed land but not labor used elimination (Wolfe): the Herero 1904 lost about 65-80%, and Tasmania, Australia and the US West saw large losses. Bantustans: about 3.5M forcibly removed 1960-83 into dumping grounds, with high mortality, a real warehouse. Gaza: aid-dependent, and each uprising was met with escalating force; over 60k deaths (about 3%) by 2025. Kenya Mau Mau: detention of tens of thousands, officially about 11k rebels killed. Pattern: once labor is unneeded and the population is openly hostile, collective punishment and containment become the default, and elimination happens when land or resources are valued and external checks are weak.
11. Does provision defuse grievance? Partly. Rentier states (Ross): transfers buy quiescence where citizens are a privileged group (Gulf). Saudi Arabia spent about $130B in 2011 to preempt the Arab Spring, and it worked. Bahrain, which excluded a sect, revolted anyway and was crushed. Cash transfers cut violence in some settings (Liberia, Blattman et al.), but aid can also draw insurgent sabotage (Crost-Felter-Johnston 2014). Reservations and deindustrialized regions: transfers prevent starvation but not despair. Mortality goes to suicide, overdose and alcohol (Case-Deaton; Native American life expectancy about 65 vs about 76 overall). After defeat, open revolt is rare (AIM 1973 is an exception). So warehousing turns revolt into slow mortality once resistance is broken, and it is unstable while the population still has capacity.

## Key parameters (full list in the workflow output)
- P(sustained violent resistance | mass livelihood loss, inadequate provision, high-capacity state) about 0.30 (0.15-0.50); weak state 0.5 (0.35-0.7).
- P(sporadic sabotage/assassination campaign | mass loss) about 0.9.
- P(state escalates repression | sustained violent resistance) about 0.9.
- P(escalation to lethal collective punishment | sustained insurgency, labor unneeded) about 0.35 (0.15-0.6).
- Provision cuts revolt hazard by about 50% (30-70%) but grievance by only about 25%.
- Warehouse excess mortality (despair/neglect) about 0.3%/yr (0.1-1.0).

## Caveats
Opposing evidence exists: grievance rarely becomes an effective revolt against high-capacity states (Depression, Xinjiang, late-phase Holodomor), and famines under a strong state rarely trigger revolt during the acute phase. "Hatred -> insurgency" is not automatic. The main lethal risk is repression escalation plus neglect, not a revolt that wins.
